# Abstract {.unnumbered #sec:abstract}

The canonical subduction zone thermal model hypothesizes that most subduction systems share two common features: slab-mantle coupling at 70--80 km depth and thin backarc lithospheres. This view is predicated on surface heat flow profiles from a small number of well-instrumented segments and implicitly assumes that those profiles are spatially representative over hundreds of km along strike. We evaluated this assumption by applying ordinary Kriging to 60,316 surface heat flow measurements across 34 subduction zone domains, comparing Krige and Similarity interpolations point-by-point, and applying cross-correlation function (CCF) analysis to 201 adjacent trench-perpendicular transect pairs. Along-strike continuity was geographically limited rather than globally uniform. Four well-sampled domains (Japan, Cascadia, Sumatra, and Kermadec--Hikurangi) showed sustained continuity consistent with observations and both interpolation methods. Three other well-sampled domains (Kurils--Kamchatka, Central America--Panama, and Ryukyus--Sagami) showed abrupt along-strike changes at 100--150 km scales, rendering single-profile characterizations misleading in those settings. Two domains (the Caribbean and Izu--Bonin) exhibited systematic method disagreement with abrupt CCF transitions whose locations could not be independently confirmed. However, most of the dataset was too sparse for any independent assessment with nearly three quarters of all transects (168/235; 71%) having 25 or fewer observations. Kriging and Similarity exhibited complementary failure modes. In well-sampled settings, Similarity mischaracterized along-strike correlations while Kriging tracked local observations closely; in sparse settings, only Similarity provided a substantive estimate of surface heat flow. Critically, method agreement did not guarantee accuracy. In Izu--Bonin, both interpolation methods returned positive correlations contradicted by 190 local observations. In the Caribbean, the two methods displaced an inferred structural boundary by roughly 100--150 km relative to its observationally inferred location. Kriging optimization converged for all 34 domains but recovered no preferred variogram model or characteristic spatial scale, consistent with heterogeneous surface heat flow patterns across subduction systems. The canonical model of subduction zone thermal structure is validated for a small, geographically biased subset of well-studied systems; whether it applies more broadly cannot be determined from the existing surface heat flow record. Answering this question requires strategically targeted surveys and hybrid Kriging--Similarity approaches guided by the failure-mode diagnostics defined here.

# Plain Language Summary {.unnumbered #sec:plain-language-summary}

When one tectonic plate dives beneath another at a subduction zone, the two plates lock together and drive flow of warm rock in Earth's mantle. Scientists measure the heat escaping through Earth's surface to locate this "sticking point" because its depth controls fault zone behavior and shapes regional earthquake hazards. For decades, the sticking point was assumed to occur consistently near 70--80 km depth---a conclusion drawn from a few heavily studied regions and built on the idea that a single cross-section could represent the thermal structure for hundreds of km along a subduction zone. We tested this assumption by analyzing more than 60,000 surface heat flow measurements across 34 subduction zone segments worldwide using two independent mapping methods. In four regions, including Japan and Cascadia, the thermal structure remained consistent along strike, validating the classic model for those specific segments. In three other well-sampled regions, the thermal structure changed abruptly over distances of 100--150 km, meaning a single cross-section cannot capture the regional picture. In two additional regions, our methods gave contradictory answers that could not be resolved with available data, and for nearly three quarters of all cross-sections examined, there were too few measurements to evaluate the thermal structure independently at all. We also found that agreement between two mapping methods does not mean either is correct. In one region, both methods indicated a uniform thermal structure while 190 ground measurements directly contradicted that conclusion. Resolving whether the classic model of subduction zone thermal structure applies globally requires strategically placed field surveys targeting specific regions, combined with mapping strategies that integrate local measurements and global geological patterns in a principled way.

# Keypoints {.unnumbered #sec:key-points}

- Along-strike surface heat flow continuity is geographically limited; four domains showed consistent continuity, three showed clear discontinuity
- Kriging and Similarity exhibit complementary failure modes; method consensus does not guarantee accuracy
- Nearly three quarters of all transects had $\leq$ 25 observations, making sparsity the dominant constraint on geodynamic interpretation

# Introduction {#sec:introduction}

Surface heat flow reflects the integrated thermal state of the lithosphere and contributions from hydrothermal circulation, radioactive decay, fault motion, and mantle convection [@fourier1827; @kelvin1863; @stein1994; @hutnak2008; @furlong2013]. Observations of surface heat flow are therefore central for understanding lithospheric evolution [@parsons1977; @pollack1977; @stein1992; @hasterok2013; @rudnick1998], subduction dynamics and seismic hazards [@furukawa1993; @wada2009; @currie2004; @currie2006; @gao2014; @kohn2018], groundwater hydrology [@stein1994; @hutnak2008], and exploration of economic resources including geothermal energy [@batir2016; @majorowicz2010]. Decades of community effort have produced tens of thousands of continental and oceanic measurements compiled into global databases [@pollack1993; @hasterok2008; @lucazeau2019; @jennings2021] that underpin multidisciplinary investigations of lithospheric and crustal processes.

A canonical model of subduction zone thermal structure has emerged from studies that calibrate numerical thermomechanical models against individual trench-perpendicular surface heat flow profiles, concluding that slab-mantle coupling near 70--80 km depth and a thin backarc lithosphere are common features of active margins [@furukawa1993; @wada2009; @hyndman2005; @currie2004; @currie2006]. This model rests on the implicit assumption that a small number of well-instrumented transects are spatially representative for hundreds of km along strike. If adjacent transects instead show markedly different surface heat flow profiles---as we hypothesized in this work---that assumption breaks down and fundamental questions arise: do subduction dynamics genuinely vary over 10--100s of km along strike, or do near-surface perturbations and observational gaps merely obscure a continuous thermal signature?

Investigating this geodynamic problem requires evaluating transect-scale surface heat flow patterns across broad regions along strike. While the most recent global compilation from the International Heat Flow Commission (IHFC 2024) contains 91,182 surface heat flow observations [@ihfc2024], regional coverage near subduction zones remains sparse and highly uneven at scales of 10--1000 km. High-resolution local surveys (often collocated with seismic monitoring networks) capture fine-scale variations at individual sites but do not resolve along-strike variability across subduction zone segments. The critical methodological challenge is therefore evaluating sparse, irregularly spaced observations separated by distances ranging from 0.1--1000 km.

To address both the geodynamic problem and methodological challenge, we evaluated two independent interpolation methods that represent end-member approaches built on fundamentally different principles: *Kriging* [@krige1951] and *Similarity* [@goutorbe2011]. Their differences are highly consequential for understanding lithospheric thermal structure, detecting surface heat flow anomalies, assessing practical limitations of each approach, and motivating the development of hybrid methods. The rationale for applying both is embodied in the *Three Laws of Geography*:

1. Everything is related, but near things are more related than distant things [@matheron1963]
2. Geographic phenomena are inherently heterogeneous [@goodchild2004]
3. Localities with similar geographic configurations share other attributes [@zhu2018]

Kriging exploits the First Law by estimating surface heat flow in data-sparse regions using a linear combination of nearby observations, weighted by their spatial correlations. This reliance on spatial correlation matches the physics of surface heat flow, which reflects large-scale lithospheric thermal structure and heat-transfer processes. For instance, broad regions of low surface heat flow on continents outline cratons [@nyblade1993]; anomalously low oceanic surface heat flow indicates heat extraction by seawater [@stein1994; @fisher2000; @hutnak2008; @hasterok2011]; and trench-perpendicular surface heat flow profiles have been interpreted as evidence for broadly uniform slab-mantle coupling depths [@furukawa1993; @wada2009] and upper-plate lithospheric thickness [@currie2004; @currie2006; @hyndman2005].

Similarity, in contrast, does not consider spatial correlations. It predicts surface heat flow at any target location by comparing that location's *geographic configuration* (the combination of geologic and geophysical attributes) against the entire global database of measured surface heat flow [@goutorbe2011]. This reflects the Third Law: localities sharing similar bathymetry, lithology, proximity to orogens, seafloor age, and upper-mantle velocity tend to produce similar surface heat flow [@chapman1975; @lee1965; @sclater1970; @davies2013; @shapiro2004; @lucazeau2019]. Similarity estimates are therefore geologically reasoned estimates rather than spatial interpolations.

These considerations motivate a direct test of the assumption underlying the canonical subduction zone thermal model: that single surface heat flow profiles are regionally representative of thermal structure, and that profiles from different subduction systems share common features. Here we asked three specific questions: a) How spatially continuous is surface heat flow near active subduction zones? b) How do Krige and Similarity estimates compare along individual transects and between adjacent transects? c) What do methodological agreements and disagreements imply about geodynamic variability and observational gaps?

Our specific implementation applied Kriging to IHFC 2024 data near 235 predefined trench-perpendicular transects (organized into 34 transect sets) from the SubMap tool [@lallemand2017; @lallemand2026], spanning four major subduction zone regions across the globe. These Krige estimates were compared point-by-point to global Similarity estimates from @lucazeau2019, and we used cross-correlation function (CCF) analysis on adjacent transects to quantify along-strike spatial continuity. The resulting analysis reveals both region-specific and method-specific patterns with implications for how surface heat flow observations constrain subduction zone dynamics.

# Methods {#sec:methods}

## Spatial Datasets {#sec:datasets}

### IHFC 2024 Surface Heat Flow Database {#sec:ihfc2024-database}

The IHFC 2024 database from the @ihfc2024 was used for Kriging and CCF analysis. The database contains 91,182 surface heat flow observations from continental and oceanic settings (@fig:global-dataset), together with geographic coordinates, elevation, unique observation identifiers, and other metadata. Measurement quality metadata are encoded using the IHFC uncertainty (U) and measurement (M) classification scheme described by the @ihfc2024.

The database was preprocessed prior to interpolation. First, observations were filtered using a simple range criterion, retaining only surface heat flow values within the interval $(0, 250]$ mW m$^{-2}$. This step excluded non-physical values and extreme observations commonly associated with localized geothermal systems or measurement artifacts [@lucazeau2019]. No additional filtering or weighting based on IHFC quality classifications was applied.

Second, duplicate observations located at identical coordinates were identified and resolved. Where duplicate records had different quality classifications, the observation with the higher combined uncertainty and measurement quality score (U + M) was retained. When duplicate observations had equal quality scores, one observation was selected randomly.

After duplicate removal and range filtering, the processed IHFC 2024 dataset contained 60,316 observations used for Kriging and CCF analysis.

### SubMap Transects and Interpolation Domains {#sec:submap-transects}

We used SubMap transects as a standardized geographic framework for sampling and comparing surface heat flow. SubMap transects are predefined trench-perpendicular cross-sections through active subduction zones [@lallemand2017; @lallemand2026]. Transects were organized into 34 geographically contiguous sets spanning four regional groups: the N Pacific (NPA; 8 sets, 47 transects), S America (SAM; 10 sets, 66 transects), SE Asia (SEA; 12 sets, 90 transects), and the SW Pacific (SWP; 4 sets, 32 transects), for a total of 235 transects (@fig:global-dataset; Tables S1, S2).

For each transect set, an interpolation domain was defined as a convex polygon enclosing all transects in the set. IHFC 2024 observations and interpolated surface heat flow grids within each domain (@fig:npa-set2-comp: top row) were sampled for CCF analysis. Surface heat flow data were projected onto each transect (@fig:npa-set2-comp: bottom row) by computing the orthogonal projection distance from each data point to the transect line. A lateral buffer of 75 km on each side of each transect (150 km total buffer width) defined the projection window, limiting contributions from points far from the transect. A total of 18,635 IHFC 2024 observations were sampled within the 34 transect set domains (Table S1) with a total of 9,325 observations located within the projection windows of the 235 transects (Table S2).

### Map Projection and Interpolation Grid {#sec:map-projection}

Spatial datasets were processed using the R package `sf` [@pebesma2018]. The IHFC 2024 observations, @lucazeau2019 Similarity interpolation, and SubMap transects were imported and stored in geographic coordinates referenced to WGS84 (EPSG:4326). Local azimuthal equidistant projections (ESRI:54032) were applied where required for geometric buffering operations during construction of convex polygons, after which geometries were transformed back to geographic coordinates referenced to WGS84 (EPSG:4326).

Target locations for Kriging were defined using the geometry of the global surface heat flow grid distributed with the Similarity interpolation of @lucazeau2019, enabling direct point-by-point comparison between Krige estimates and Similarity estimates (e.g., see @fig:npa-set2-comp: top row).

## Kriging {#sec:kriging}

Ordinary Kriging was applied to IHFC 2024 observations within each convex polygon domain using the R package `gstat` [@graler2016; @pebesma2004]. Kriging is a three-step process that involves: 1) computing an experimental variogram $\hat{\gamma}(h)$ to characterize spatial correlations, 2) fitting a variogram model $\gamma(h)$ to the experimental variogram $\hat{\gamma}(h)$, and 3) solving a linear system of equations to predict surface heat flow at target locations [@krige1951; @cressie2015; @matheron1963]. The experimental variogram was computed as:

$$
  \hat{\gamma}(h) = \frac{1}{2N(h)} \sum_{i,j \in N(h)} \Big[Z(u_i) - Z(u_j)\Big]^2
$$ {#eq:variogram}

where $Z(u_i)$ and $Z(u_j)$ are observations at locations $u_i$ and $u_j$ separated by lag $h = \lVert u_i - u_j \rVert$, and $N(h)$ is the number of observation pairs separated by that lag distance. For the irregularly spaced data in this study, $N(h)$ was defined using a bin width $\delta = \max(h)\, /\, \kappa\, n$ such that:

$$
  N(h) = \# \left\{(i,j) : h - \frac{\delta}{2} \le h_{ij} < h + \frac{\delta}{2} \right\}
$$ {#eq:pairs}

where $\max(h)$ is the maximum separation distance within the Kriging domain, $n$ is the number of lag intervals, and $\kappa$ is the lag cutoff constant that limits the maximum lag distance $h$ used to construct the experimental variogram $\hat{\gamma}(h)$ (conventionally $\kappa$ = 3). Kriging was applied locally by restricting each estimate to the $m$ nearest observations that fell within a predefined maximum neighborhood size $d_\mathrm{max}$, allowing the effective interpolation radius to adapt to local data density.

Rather than choosing variogram parameters by eye (a common but subjective practice), we simultaneously optimized all parameters using constrained nonlinear optimization [after @li2018]. The parameters $\{n,\, \kappa,\, m,\, d_\mathrm{max}\}$ were tuned to minimize a weighted cost function combining variogram-fitting error and cross-validation interpolation error. Global optimization used the MLSL algorithm with Nelder-Mead local refinement. A subsequent fine-tuning step searched a restricted parameter neighborhood; both stages were implemented in the R package `nloptr`. Full optimization details and results for all transect sets are presented in the Supplementary Information (Text S1; Figures S5--S38; Table S9).

## Cross-Correlation Function Analysis {#sec:cross-correlation-function-analysis}

CCF analysis measured how consistently surface heat flow patterns varied along strike within each transect set. First, surface heat flow data from IHFC 2024 observations, Similarity interpolation, and Krige interpolation were projected onto each transect. To extract continuous trench-perpendicular trends, these projected profiles were smoothed using generalized additive models [GAMs, @wood2017] via the `mgcv` package in R. Smooth profile curves were modeled as a function of along-transect distance using thin plate regression splines, with the smoothing parameter optimized via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML). The default basis dimension constraint was applied, allowing the algorithm to dynamically penalize overfitting based on sample size. Trend fitting was restricted to transects containing a minimum of 10 projected data points. Finally, the smoothed mean profiles were resampled to 500 evenly spaced points along each transect, running from the subducting plate to approximately 500 km into the upper plate (e.g., see @fig:npa-set2-comp: bottom row).

For each pair of adjacent transects, the similarity between surface heat flow profiles was quantified using the `stats::ccf()` function in R. The analysis allowed small horizontal shifts between profiles (up to $\pm$ 10% of the total transect length) to account for minor offsets in the positions of surface heat flow anomalies. The maximum correlation value was retained as a measure of profile similarity. Correlation coefficients near 1 indicate that adjacent transects have very similar surface heat flow patterns, whereas values near 0 or negative indicate weakly related or contrasting patterns.

CCF analysis was performed for all three surface heat flow datasets: IHFC 2024 observations (where data coverage was sufficient), Similarity interpolation, and Krige interpolation. Additional comparisons between methods (Obs--Sim, Obs--Krg, and Sim--Krg) were computed for individual transects to evaluate agreement between interpolation approaches. In total, 201 adjacent transect pairs were analyzed across the four regional groups (39 in NPA, 56 in SAM, 78 in SEA, and 28 in SWP).

Following the pair-wise calculations, transect sets were classified into four spatial regimes based on data density and profile correlation thresholds. Sets with a mean density of fewer than 25 observations per transect were classified as ***ambiguous***. For sets meeting the 25-observation threshold, classifications were determined by summarizing cross-method agreement and along-strike continuity:

- ***Inferred continuous***: required high method agreement within individual transects (mean Obs--Krg CCF $\geq$ 0.70) and high along-strike agreement across all data types (minimum Obs CCF and Krg CCF and Sim CCF $\geq$ 0.50)
- ***Inferred discontinuous***: assigned when methods agreed within individual transects (mean Obs--Krg CCF $\geq$ 0.70) but adjacent profiles revealed abrupt spatial shifts in either the observations (minimum Obs CCF < 0) or Krige estimates (minimum Krg CCF < 0.30)
- ***Method disagreement***: assigned when estimates conflicted, either through poor agreement between observations and Krige estimates across individual transect (mean Obs--Krg CCF < 0.30) or when observations caught a structural boundary (minimum Obs CCF < 0) that the Similarity method smoothed over (maximum Sim CCF > 0.50).

Any remaining sets that did not fit these definitive criteria were designated as ***ambiguous***.

## Interpolation Accuracy {#sec:interpolation-accuracy}

Interpolation accuracy was assessed by pairing estimated surface heat flow values from the $0.5^\circ \times 0.5^\circ$ interpolation grid directly with the IHFC 2024 observations [after @lucazeau2019]. To minimize spatial mismatch, point pairs were established using a bidirectional nearest-neighbor approach: the nearest observation to each grid node, and the nearest grid node to each observation, were cross-referenced. Pairs separated by a Euclidean distance exceeding a strict 10 km threshold were excluded from the analysis to ensure localized validity.

The final evaluation dataset mapped each qualified interpolated node to its nearest valid observation. Estimation errors across the SubMap transect sets were then summarized using standard descriptive metrics, including the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), mean error, and interquartile range (IQR).

The same evaluation procedure was applied to both Similarity and Krige interpolations to ensure consistent comparison between methods. Cross-validation approaches commonly used for Kriging were not adopted because the Similarity method uses the full global dataset and is less sensitive to removal of individual observations [@goutorbe2011]. Direct comparison with observations therefore provided the most consistent basis for evaluating both interpolation methods.

# Results {#sec:results}

## Along-Strike CCF Analysis Comparing Adjacent Transect Pairs {#sec:along-strike-ccf-analysis-comparing-adjacent-transect-pairs}

CCF analysis across 201 adjacent transect pairs revealed substantial variation in along-strike surface heat flow continuity among subduction systems (@fig:global-ccf-summary; Table S7). The classifications shown in @fig:global-ccf-summary summarize the dominant behavior of each transect set rather than requiring perfect agreement at every transect pair. A classification therefore reflects the prevailing surface heat flow pattern across a region, while local departures are examined separately in @sec:transect-level-ccf-analysis-comparing-observations-kriging-and-similarity.

Four domains exhibited sustained along-strike continuity over hundreds of km. Japan (NPA_SET1), Cascadia (NPA_SET8), Sumatra (SEA_SET2), and Kermadec--Hikurangi (SWP_SET4) all combined moderate-to-high observation density with strong agreement among observations, Kriging, and Similarity estimates (@fig:global-ccf-summary: green shaded areas; Table S7). In these regions, neighboring transects consistently reproduced similar surface heat flow patterns despite spanning large distances along strike. These domains therefore provide the clearest evidence that spatially continuous thermal structure can persist across substantial portions of some subduction segments.

Three well-sampled domains instead exhibited abrupt changes in apparent thermal structure between neighboring transects. Kurils--Kamchatka (NPA_SET3), Central America--Panama (SAM_SET2), and Ryukyus--Sagami (SEA_SET9) all contained adjacent transects with weak or negative correlations that were supported by both observations and Krige profiles (@fig:global-ccf-summary: red shaded areas; Table S7). In Central America--Panama, strongly correlated transects occurred immediately adjacent to discontinuous pairs, demonstrating that surface heat flow continuity could change over distances comparable to a single transect spacing. Manila (SEA_SET7) displayed a similar transition from continuity to discontinuity, although relatively sparse observations limited independent validation. Together, these domains indicate that along-strike thermal structure is not uniformly continuous and may change abruptly over distances of roughly 100--150 km.

Two domains exhibited mixed continuity patterns that could not be attributed confidently to either continuity or discontinuity. The Caribbean (SAM_SET4) and Izu--Bonin (SEA_SET10) both contained alternating patterns of high and low adjacent-transect similarity, with observations and interpolation methods supporting different interpretations of the same region (@fig:global-ccf-summary: yellow shaded areas; Table S7). These patterns suggest either unresolved thermal heterogeneity at scales smaller than the interpolation grid or methodological limitations in reconstructing local surface heat flow structure. Consequently, the location and significance of apparent thermal regime boundaries remained uncertain in both domains.

Most transect sets remained ambiguous because observation density was too low to reliably evaluate continuity. Large portions of Alaska, the Caribbean--Andean margin, SE Asia, and the SW Pacific contained transects with few or no observations (Tables S1, S2), preventing independent validation of interpolated profiles. In these regions, Krige estimates were highly sensitive to sparse local data, while Similarity provided the only spatially complete surface heat flow estimate. As a result, continuity, discontinuity, and method disagreement could not be inferred confidently across 71--75% of the global subduction zone system examined here (Table S7).

## Transect-Level CCF Analysis Comparing Observations, Kriging, and Similarity {#sec:transect-level-ccf-analysis-comparing-observations-kriging-and-similarity}

The classifications in @fig:global-ccf-summary describe the dominant continuity pattern within each transect set, but they do not imply perfect agreement among observations and interpolation methods at every transect. Examination of individual transect pairs (@fig:method-ccf-summary) revealed three recurring sources of uncertainty: 1) disagreement between Similarity and locally constrained data, 2) Kriging failure despite dense observations, and 3) increasing interpolation uncertainty in sparsely sampled regions.

The most common disagreement occurred when Similarity produced continuity estimates inconsistent with both observations and Krige profiles. The clearest example occurred in Ryukyus--Sagami (SEA_SET9), where observation density was high ($N_\mathrm{obs}$ = 83--279) and Obs--Krg agreement was consistently strong (Obs--Krg CCF = 0.70--1.00; @fig:method-ccf-summary; Table S8). Across five consecutive transect pairs, observations and Kriging agreed on both the sign and magnitude of adjacent-transect correlations, while Similarity returned a different interpretation for four of the five pairs (@fig:sea67-sea68-sea69-sea70-sea71-sea72-composition; Table S7). In several cases, Similarity indicated strong continuity where observations and Kriging indicated discontinuity, whereas in another pair it failed to reproduce a nearly perfect correlation identified by both locally-constrained datasets. Because observations and Kriging converged across multiple neighboring transects, the disagreement is attributed to Similarity failing to resolve the apparent local thermal structure. Ryukyus--Sagami therefore illustrates a failure mode in which geologically derived Similarity estimates diverged from observationally constrained surface heat flow patterns.

The same failure mode appeared in several independent subduction systems. In Central America--Panama (SAM_SET2), Similarity underestimated continuity in one transect pair and overestimated it in the immediately adjacent pair, despite strong support from observations and Kriging for opposite outcomes. Similar discrepancies occurred in Japan (NPA_SET1), where Kriging closely tracked observations while Similarity consistently returned weaker correlations, and in Kurils--Kamchatka (NPA_SET3), where Similarity indicated strong continuity despite both observations and Kriging indicating discontinuity (Figures [-@fig:global-ccf-summary; -@fig:method-ccf-summary]; Tables S7, S8). The recurrence of these disagreements across multiple regions suggests that Similarity sometimes failed to reproduce along-strike thermal structure even where local observations were sufficient to constrain it.

A second failure mode occurred when dense observations did not produce reliable Krige profiles. In most well-sampled domains, Kriging closely reproduced observed thermal structure and Obs--Krg CCF values were high. However, two domains departed from this pattern. In the Caribbean (SAM_SET4), the most densely sampled transects in the region (SAM31, SAM32; $N_\mathrm{obs}$ = 129, 175) exhibited Obs--Krg CCF values near zero, indicating that the interpolated Krige profiles bore little resemblance to the underlying observations (@fig:method-ccf-summary; Table S8). A similar result occurred in Izu--Bonin (SEA_SET10), where one densely sampled transect (SEA73; $N_\mathrm{obs}$ = 190) also produced near-zero Obs--Krg agreement. For the adjacent pair SEA73--SEA74, both interpolation methods indicated positive continuity, yet observations indicated weak discontinuity (Table S7). These examples demonstrate that high observation density alone did not guarantee accurate Krige-based profiles or continuity estimates.

Interpolation uncertainty increased markedly where observations were sparse. Point-by-point Sim--Krg prediction differences were centered near zero in most domains, indicating little systematic bias between methods, but the spread of those differences varied substantially among regions (Figure S1; Table S3). The largest discrepancies occurred in the Andean domains SAM_SET9 and SAM_SET10 and in New Hebrides (SWP_SET2), where transects contained few observations and Krige variance was correspondingly large. Similarity uncertainty remained lower than Krige uncertainty across most domains (Figure S2; Table S4), whereas in well-sampled regions Kriging generally produced narrower residual distributions and stronger agreement with observations (Figures S3, S4; Tables S5, S6). These patterns indicate that uncertainty in surface heat flow estimates increased systematically as observational constraints weakened.

## Optimized Variogram Parameters Across the 34 Domains {#sec:optimized-variogram-parameters-across-the-34-domains}

Optimized Krige parameters varied widely among transect sets and showed no evidence for a single characteristic spatial scale of surface heat flow continuity in subduction zones. All 34 domains converged to an optimal numerical solution (Figures S5--S38; Table S9). While this consistent convergence demonstrates the mathematical stability of the optimization algorithm across a full range of data densities, we note that the resulting variograms in observation-poor domains (per-transect mean $N_\mathrm{obs}$ density $\leq$ 25) are highly unconstrained and subject to severe parameter volatility, reflecting localized sample noise rather than a physically meaningful spatial structure. Nevertheless, the optimization reliably handled the full spectrum of sampling conditions, from dense networks like Cascadia (up to 1,077 observations per transect) to sparse regions with no immediate transect observations. Exponential, spherical, and Gaussian variogram models were each selected across multiple domains; maximum neighborhood sizes $d_\mathrm{max}$ ranged from approximately 100 to 500 km; lag cutoff constants $\kappa$ ranged from 3--12; and the number of lag intervals $n$ ranged from 20 to nearly 50 (@fig:nlopt-summary; Table S9). Domains with comparable observation counts frequently converged on different variogram models and spatial scales, and similarly low-cost solutions arose from a wide range of parameter configurations. No systematic relationship existed between individual parameter values and the total optimization cost.

# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## Along-Strike Surface Heat Flow Continuity is Ambiguous or Geographically Limited {#sec:along-strike-heat-flow-continuity-is-ambiguous-or-geographically-limited}

Numerical thermomechanical models of subduction zones have historically used individual trench-perpendicular surface heat flow profiles to calibrate their initial thermal structures and boundary conditions. @wada2009 synthesized models across 17 subduction zones and concluded that a common slab-mantle coupling depth near 70--80 km reconciles volcanic arc positions and forearc surface heat flow globally. @hyndman2005 and @currie2006 identified broadly uniform backarc surface heat flow of approximately 75 $\pm$ 15 mW m$^{-2}$ across ten circum-Pacific systems, attributing this uniformity to vigorous slab-driven corner flow and a thin, thermally eroded backarc lithosphere. These conclusions rest implicitly on the assumption that the selected surface heat flow profiles are spatially continuous for hundreds of km along strike. The CCF analysis conducted here directly evaluated that assumption across 201 adjacent transect pairs.

Our CCF analysis results support the canonical model of subduction zone thermal structure only for a subset of segments where those models were originally calibrated. Japan (NPA_SET1), Cascadia (NPA_SET8), Sumatra (SEA_SET2), and Kermadec--Hikurangi (SWP_SET4) all showed sustained along-strike continuity (Obs CCF = 0.56--1.00), indicating that only 24--40% of trench-perpendicular profiles underlying the @wada2009, @hyndman2005, and @currie2006 syntheses were regionally representative. Their tacit assumption that a single profile captures the broader thermal state of a subduction segment was therefore geographically limited. Elsewhere it did not hold. Three well-sampled domains (Kurils--Kamchatka: NPA_SET3, Central America--Panama: SAM_SET2, and Ryukyus--Sagami: SEA_SET9) showed abrupt along-strike changes at 100--150 km scales, which would render single-profile characterizations misleading. Two additional domains (Caribbean: SAM_SET4; Izu--Bonin: SEA_SET10) showed abrupt CCF transitions whose locations could not be reliably determined due to method disagreement, and 71--75% of the global dataset lacked sufficient observations for independent assessment (Figures [-@fig:global-ccf-summary; -@fig:method-ccf-summary]; Tables S7, S8). The canonical model of uniform coupling-depths with thin backarc lithospheres is therefore better understood as a locally validated description of a small number of well-characterized segments than as a common feature of active subduction zones worldwide.

Several deep-seated tectonic mechanisms could explain along-strike deviations from the canonical model of subduction zone thermal structure. Numerical geodynamic modeling studies have shown that variations in slab geometry can concentrate hot subarc mantle at specific segments via dynamic pressure gradients [@araya2021], and that cessation of backarc spreading can trigger lithospheric instabilities and along-strike temperature heterogeneity over million-year timescales [@lee2021]. @kerswell2021 showed that backarc lithospheric thickness and serpentinite stability jointly control the maximum slab-mantle coupling depth, offering another physical mechanism for the along-strike variability resolved by our CCF analysis, while @peacock2021 proposed a related mineralogical control involving the pressure-dependent breakdown of talc near 2.0--2.5 GPa (70--80 km depth). Subduction interface friction may also matter. @kohn2018 showed that forearc surface heat flow requires shear heating with friction coefficients of 0.03--0.1, so along-strike variation in friction or convergence rate could similarly produce the surface heat flow variability documented here. Where slab geometry, metamorphic dehydration depth, or interface friction vary along strike, adjacent profiles can record different thermal regimes within a single subduction segment.

Yet each of these deeper mechanisms could in principle be mimicked at the surface by near-surface hydrothermal perturbations. Hydrothermal circulation in young oceanic crust can reduce local conductive measurements to 10--40% of lithospheric predictions [@hutnak2008], and channelized basement fluid flow can create focused surface heat flow anomalies over lateral scales of tens of km [@fisher2000; @fisher2010]. Without collocated seismic velocity models, fluid pressure data, or sediment thickness constraints, surface heat flow cannot discriminate between a slab-mantle coupling depth transition and a locally perturbed hydrothermal signal. This ambiguity is an inherent limitation of surface heat flow as a geophysical observable in tectonically active settings.

## Interpolation Failure Modes Depend on Observation Density and Proxy Resolution {#sec:interpolation-failure-modes-depend-on-observation-density-and-proxy-resolution}

The three uncertainty patterns identified in our CCF analysis (Sim--Obs disagreement, Kriging failure in dense settings, and sparsity-driven uncertainty; see @sec:transect-level-ccf-analysis-comparing-observations-kriging-and-similarity) reflect a systematic dependence of method reliability on observation density. Where per-transect observations were sparse, Krige profiles overfit individual measurements, leaving Similarity as the only substantive estimate. Where observations were dense, Kriging closely reproduced local data while Similarity mischaracterized along-strike correlations in specific settings. Understanding when and where each method can fail is essential for interpreting the surface heat flow record across the range of data densities represented in the IHFC 2024 dataset (Table S2).

In sparse domains, per-transect observation counts of roughly 25 or fewer produced Krige profiles sensitive to individual measurements rather than regional surface heat flow structure. The Aleutian domain (NPA_SET5) illustrated this clearly: 4--12 observations per transect yielded highly variable Krg CCF across seven consecutive transect pairs, while Similarity produced high correlations for six of the seven (@fig:global-ccf-summary; Table S7). In such domains, Similarity provides the only available geological interpolation. However, @lucazeau2019 validated Similarity estimates at the global scale; systematic validation at the 100--150 km transect-pair scale has not been previously demonstrated. Whether Similarity accurately captures along-strike thermal structure within a single subduction segment therefore remains ambiguous. Where local observations are absent, any failure of the Similarity method at the transect scale would go undetected.

In well-sampled domains, the failure-mode pattern reversed and exposed a specific limitation of Similarity. In Ryukyus--Sagami (SEA_SET9), observations and Kriging converged on consistent along-strike CCF values across five consecutive transect pairs, yet Similarity returned discordant values for four of the five, including misidentifying discontinuous segments as continuous and substantially underestimating a near-perfect correlation inferred by both observations and Krige estimates (@fig:sea67-sea68-sea69-sea70-sea71-sea72-composition; Table S7). Central America--Panama (SAM_SET2) demonstrated that Similarity failures can occur in both directions within immediately adjacent pairs---underestimating strong continuity inferred by observations and Kriging in one pair and returning strong continuity where observations indicated a structural discontinuity in the next (Figures [-@fig:global-ccf-summary; -@fig:method-ccf-summary]; Tables S7, S8). Similar discrepancies in Japan and Kurils--Kamchatka confirm that these failures are not confined to a single region.

These systematic failures reflect how Similarity constructs its estimates. The method identifies global analogs in multivariate proxy space and applies weighted averaging to their measured values [@goutorbe2011]. Adjacent transects within a single subduction segment may share nearly identical proxy configurations while having distinct along-strike thermal structure. Such differences can arise where fine-scale slab roughness (i.e., interface friction), localized fluid pathways, or short-wavelength variations in deeper mantle wedge flow are not represented by any proxy variable. The 0.5$^\circ$ $\times$ 0.5$^\circ$ grid spacing of the @lucazeau2019 implementation (approximately 55 km at equatorial latitudes) may further prevent the proxy field from resolving along-strike thermal gradients at the transect scale. Prior validation by @shapiro2004 demonstrated the viability of proxy-guided interpolation at regional scales but did not consider the resolution-dependent limitation identified here.

The Kriging failures in the Caribbean (SAM_SET4) and Izu--Bonin (SEA_SET10) illustrate a complementary limitation. At those transects, thermal structure varied at spatial scales shorter than any variogram model's correlation length could resolve. Long variogram ranges over-smoothed the profiles, whereas short ranges were incompatible with sparse data coverage elsewhere in the domain. No single variogram configuration reconciled these competing constraints. The convergence of different Krige parameter configurations across domains therefore reflects regional differences in the scale of surface heat flow organization rather than degeneracy in the optimization algorithm (see Text S1 for full algorithm details). Across the 34 subduction domains examined here, the absence of a preferred variogram model or characteristic length scale (@fig:nlopt-summary; Table S9) further suggests that surface heat flow is heterogeneous at all resolvable scales and effectively unconstrained below a critical observation-density threshold.

## Method Agreement Does Not Guarantee Accuracy {#sec:method-agreement-does-not-guarantee-accuracy}

The failure modes described in the previous section share an important diagnostic signal: method disagreement indicates a problem, but method agreement does not guarantee accuracy. Two additional patterns in our analysis make this clear and carry direct implications for how assessments of subduction zone thermal structure based on surface heat flow should be used.

The most consequential of these is false consensus between independent interpolation methods. In Izu--Bonin (SEA_SET10), Similarity and Kriging both returned positive along-strike continuity (CCF = 0.34--0.93) for a transect pair (SEA73--SEA74) where 190 local observations indicated weak discontinuity (CCF = -0.23; @fig:global-ccf-summary; Table S7). The Krige profile at the more densely sampled of the two transects (SEA73) failed to track observed surface heat flow structure despite the high observation count (@fig:method-ccf-summary; Table S8), indicating that local thermal structure varied at scales shorter than the variogram could resolve. High Sim--Krg CCF agreement masked this failure rather than flagging it. Neither Similarity's global proxy resolution nor Kriging's regionally optimized variogram length scale captured finer-scale heterogeneity, and examining the observations directly was the only way to detect it. Analyses of this segment relying solely on interpolated estimates would have found consistent method agreement supporting a continuity interpretation that the measurements directly contradict.

The Caribbean (SAM_SET4) documented the distinct complication of apparent boundary displacement. Observations located an abrupt thermal regime boundary at one transect pair (SAM28--SAM29; Obs CCF = -0.73), while Similarity placed it at the immediately adjacent pair (SAM27--SAM28; Sim CCF = -0.61)---an apparent displacement of roughly 100--150 km (@fig:global-ccf-summary). In thermal modeling contexts where surface heat flow profiles calibrate slab-mantle coupling depths, a positional error of this magnitude is geodynamically significant. It misrepresents the lateral extent of the coupled interface and the along-strike position of key frictional or metamorphic phase transitions [e.g., @gao2014; @kohn2018; @peacock2021]. The Caribbean's multiple subduction geometries, polarity reversals, and active backarc basins [@lallemand2017] generate short-wavelength thermal variability that proxy-based methods cannot resolve at the transect scale, and dense local measurements are required to accurately locate structural boundaries.

Together, these cases establish that method agreement narrows the range of possible failure modes but does not guarantee accuracy. In well-constrained domains, agreement between (locally validated) Krige estimates and (observationally consistent) Similarity estimates carries meaningful confidence. Where neither method can be independently checked against dense local observations, however, agreement may reflect coincident or correlated failures rather than shared accuracy. The diagnostic framework developed here---evaluating per-transect observation counts combined with CCF analysis---provides a rubric for distinguishing these situations systematically. Interpolation comparison without observation-density context is an insufficient guide to interpretation.

## Observation Sparsity Constrains Geodynamic Interpretation {#sec:observation-sparsity-constrains-geodynamic-interpretation}

The transect-level observation counts reported here give a long-recognized data coverage problem a practical threshold for evaluating interpolation reliability. Nearly three quarters of all transects (168/235; 71%) lacked sufficient observations for independent validation ($N_\mathrm{obs}$ $\leq$ 25), meaning that the surface heat flow record cannot currently support or refute geodynamic inferences about along-strike continuity for most of the global subduction system. This gap reflects a persistent sampling bias toward accessible continental margins, onshore monitoring networks, and geothermal exploration targets, leaving subduction zone forearc and backarc regions systematically undersampled at the scales geodynamic interpretation requires.

Database growth has not yet resolved this issue. The IHFC 2024 compilation represents a substantial addition to prior datasets (adding roughly 21,000 measurements since the @lucazeau2019 dataset), yet the new observations are concentrated in already well-sampled regions. The along-strike gaps identified here for the Aleutians, most of S America, and much of SE Asia and the SW Pacific persist, and the $>$ 1000 km nearest-neighbor distances documented by @pollack1993 across the S Pacific remain relevant for many of the transects examined here.

Where observations do exist, systematic bias in specific tectonic settings compounds the sparsity problem. Hydrothermal advection accounts for a substantial fraction of total oceanic heat loss in young oceanic crust, and conductive measurements in these settings systematically underestimate the true lithospheric heat flux [@stein1994]. More than half of the plates entering the trenches analyzed here (127/235 transects; 54%) carry oceanic crust younger than the 65 $\pm$ 10 Ma sealing age beyond which hydrothermal circulation no longer measurably affects conductive surface heat flow [@stein1994]. Basement fluid discharge along mid-plate seamount chains can locally reduce regional conductive measurements to a small fraction of lithospheric predictions [@hutnak2008]. In forearcs and incoming-plate regions, the available observations are therefore doubly limited: too few to constrain spatial patterns, and potentially biased by hydrothermal extraction toward underestimating the thermal signal that interpolation methods attempt to reconstruct.

These limitations directly constrain geodynamic inference from the surface heat flow record. The canonical model of subduction zone thermal structure hypothesized by @wada2009 and @currie2006 was built on a small number of well-sampled sites (Japan, Kamchatka, Cascadia, and Kermadec). Our CCF analysis showed that three of those sites exhibit high along-strike continuity, confirming previous single-profile characterizations as regionally representative. Our analysis also identified one exception: Kurils--Kamchatka (NPA_SET3) showed along-strike discontinuity, indicating those profiles were less representative than previously assumed by @wada2009. For the broader global system, whether the canonical models extend beyond the four inferred continuous segments in our CCF analysis (@fig:global-ccf-summary: green shaded areas; Table S7) cannot be resolved from the existing surface heat flow record (@fig:global-ccf-summary; Table S7).

## Targeted Surveys and Hybrid Approaches {#sec:targeted-surveys-and-hybrid-approaches}

The data limitations and failure-mode diagnostics discussed above together define specific, actionable survey priorities rather than a generic call for more data. Two target categories emerge, each requiring different survey strategies and offering different scientific returns.

The most widespread case involves sparse domains where Similarity provides the only interpolation estimate and no independent validation is possible. These regions (@fig:global-ccf-summary: gray areas; Table S7) currently have no basis for evaluating whether Similarity CCF values reflect genuine thermal continuity or simply the spatial smoothness of the proxy field. Even modest targeted surveys in these regions would provide the first independent test of Similarity estimates. The per-transect density thresholds identified here define the minimum observation density needed to cross from ambiguous to interpretable (roughly $N_\mathrm{obs}$ $>$ 25).

A more demanding strategy is required where fine-scale thermal heterogeneity exists at scales below global interpolation resolution. The Caribbean and Izu--Bonin segments demonstrated that short-wavelength thermal variability can produce abrupt along-strike CCF transitions within consecutive transect pairs (Figures [-@fig:global-ccf-summary; -@fig:method-ccf-summary]; Table S7, S8), and that neither interpolation method reliably locates structural boundaries under these conditions. Resolving finer-scale structure requires closely spaced measurements rather than scattered additions, and the tectonic complexity of these systems likely also demands collocated seismic imaging and geochemical fluid sampling to separate deep thermal signals from shallow hydrothermal overprinting.

Beyond survey design, the complementary failure modes of Similarity and Kriging motivate hybrid interpolation strategies. The most direct combination treats Similarity as a geologically informed prior for the regional surface heat flow field and applies Kriging to update that prior with local measurements---conceptually equivalent to Similarity as a trend model in universal Kriging or as a secondary covariate in a co-Kriging framework. The failure-mode diagnostics define where each approach should dominate: Similarity should govern where local observations are too sparse for Kriging to produce stable estimates, while Krige estimates should take precedence where per-transect counts and Obs--Krg CCF indicate strong local constraint. A framework incorporating the @lucazeau2019 proxy variables as spatially varying covariates within a Kriging system would operationalize this without requiring separate comparison steps.

Kriging and Similarity also perform optimally at different spatial scales and can be deployed accordingly. The Ryukyus--Sagami analysis showed that Kriging outperformed Similarity at the transect-pair (@fig:sea67-sea68-sea69-sea70-sea71-sea72-composition; Table S7). A scale-decomposed approach could assign Similarity to characterize the large-scale background field while Kriging recovers short-scale departures where local data constrain the variogram. Within the CCF framework, this decomposition would partition along-strike continuity patterns between the geological-analog signal in the Similarity field and the locally constrained spatial structure in Krige residuals, making failure-mode attribution explicit rather than inferred.

# Conclusions {#sec:conclusions}

We applied ordinary Kriging to IHFC 2024 data across 235 transects in 34 transect-set domains and quantified along-strike continuity for 201 adjacent transect pairs using cross-correlation function (CCF) analysis. Krige estimates were compared with the global Similarity interpolation from @lucazeau2019 to evaluate where both methods provided independent, locally constrained assessments of surface heat flow structure. The combined analysis yielded four principal conclusions.

Along-strike surface heat flow continuity is ambiguous or geographically limited. Four domains (Japan, Cascadia, Sumatra, and Kermadec--Hikurangi) showed sustained continuity consistent with observations and both interpolation methods, validating the canonical model of subduction zone thermal structure for those segments [@wada2009; @currie2006]. Three well-sampled domains (Kurils--Kamchatka, Central America--Panama, and Ryukyus--Sagami) showed abrupt structural changes at 100--150 km along-strike scales independent of any interpolation method. The Caribbean and Izu--Bonin exhibited systematic method disagreement with abrupt along-strike CCF transitions whose locations could not be confirmed, and most of the global dataset lacked sufficient observations for any independent assessment.

Kriging and Similarity exhibit complementary failure modes. In sparse domains, Kriging was unreliable and Similarity provided the only substantive estimate. In well-sampled domains, Kriging tracked observations closely while Similarity mischaracterized along-strike correlations in specific settings. Method consensus did not guarantee accuracy; the diagnostic framework developed here provides a basis for distinguishing reliable from misleading interpretations.

No universal variogram model or characteristic spatial scale described subduction zone surface heat flow continuity globally. Kriging optimization converged for all 34 domains, yet exponential, spherical, and Gaussian models were each selected, and maximum search radii spanned approximately 100 to 500 km with no systematic relationship to optimization cost. Where Krige profiles failed to track local observations, thermal heterogeneity existed at spatial scales shorter than any variogram model could resolve. Convergence to different configurations across domains reflected real regional differences in the scale of surface heat flow organization rather than algorithmic degeneracy.

Observation sparsity is the dominant constraint on geodynamic interpretation across most of the global subduction system. Nearly three quarters of all transects (168/235; 71%) had 25 or fewer observations, precluding Krige-based validation for the majority of the dataset. Additional observations in the IHFC 2024 database relative to prior compilations are concentrated in already well-sampled regions. In forearc regions, sparse observations may additionally be biased toward underestimating the true lithospheric heat flux due to hydrothermal extraction [@stein1994; @hutnak2008]. Whether uniform slab-mantle coupling depths and thin backarc lithospheres are common features of subduction zones remains an open question. Answering it will require strategically targeted surface heat flow surveys guided by the failure-mode diagnostics and observation-density thresholds defined here.
